## About me

I got into security from the IT side. For close to four years I was the only IT and security person at a live-events company, which meant I was the one setting up MFA, patching devices, and keeping the payment network separate from guest Wi-Fi. Nobody was checking my work, so I started studying to find out what I was getting wrong. That led to the CompTIA certs, then the SANS program, then the GIAC exams.

Since then I have worked a help desk role across three schools in a K-12 district and co-founded an online marketplace where I handle the technical and security side. I like the defensive, practical end of the field most: figuring out what happened from logs and traffic, and closing the gap that let it happen.

## What I took from it

The Individual Game showed me where I actually stand. Scanning, forensics, and traffic analysis went well. Web application exploitation did not: I scored zero in that module, and I ran out of time on most of the password cracking challenges. Those are the two areas I am working on next.

The biggest lesson was about method more than tools. The challenges I got right were the ones where I slowed down, read the question twice, and wrote down what I knew before touching a command. The ones I got wrong were usually a misread question or an answer submitted before I had checked it. Accuracy counts in NCL, and it counts more in real work.

The Team Game was a different skill. Most of it was coordination: claiming a challenge, saying clearly when I was stuck, and writing my method down so teammates could follow it. I joined partway through, picked up one hard challenge, and saw how much faster a team moves when everyone documents as they go.

I used AI tools during the games, which NCL permits, alongside the standard ones: Nmap, Wireshark, tcpdump, hashcat, John the Ripper, and CyberChef.
